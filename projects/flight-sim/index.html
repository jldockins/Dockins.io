DIY Flight Sim

In progress — last worked 2026-09-01

What this is

What this is: A home flight-simulator cockpit built around a 1980–81 Cessna 152 II — a traditional six-pack rendered on a monitor behind a physical panel overlay, with real controls for anything a pilot would physically touch.

Done looks like: Hand-fly a full ILS from a real approach plate, in the chair, with all six instruments driven live by the simulator. No keyboard, no mouse.

Why

The motivation: Currency and procedure practice — scan, holds, approaches, missed approaches — cheaper and more available than renting. Today that practice costs a booked aircraft or a commercial sim, so it happens on someone else's schedule and rarely. The path also runs through CFII, so this is eventually a platform to instruct from, not only to train in. Both at once: practice now, instruct later.

What changes when it's done: Approaches get flown at home, regularly. Procedure practice becomes a weeknight thing rather than a scheduled, paid rental — which means it actually happens instead of being deferred to the next rental slot.

Who it's for: Me. A single-user rig. Note this sits alongside the right-seat constraint below — the instructor in that seat is one teaching me, not a student I'm teaching. Revisit this line if the CFII lands and students start sitting in it.

Non-goals — what this is deliberately NOT:

The load-bearing part. Drift rarely contradicts the motivation; it arrives as something that plausibly serves it. Naming what's out of scope is what makes drift visible.

  • Not a full glass suite. The primary scan is the six-pack; a G1000-style panel trains the wrong scan and is out. A G5 or GI 275 overlay is a separate, deferred question and is NOT excluded by this — the aircraft being purchased has dual G5s.
  • Not a motion platform — no actuators, no frame that moves. Instrument procedure is what's being practiced; motion adds cost and complexity to something the scan doesn't need.
  • Not a 182 or anything larger — the reference aircraft is a 152 and stays a 152. Bigger airframes bring back the prop control, cowl flaps and turbo that the 152 decision deliberately removed.
  • Not a certifiable device. No hour flown in this rig counts toward any rating, for me or for anyone I later instruct — the approval attaches to specific qualified hardware and this is not that. The value is proficiency, not logged time. Pursuing BATD approval is a separate open question and would be a different project.

Known temptations: The specific upgrades and detours I can already feel coming. Writing them down now makes them recognizable later.

  • A GTN 750 instead of the 650Xi — bigger, nicer, and not what the panel is specced around. A 152 panel is a tight fit as it is.
  • A second monitor for scenery — out-the-window view is not what an instrument rig needs, and it competes with the panel for budget and desk space.
  • A nicer yoke, bought before the panel exists — buying controls ahead of the thing they mount to, when the panel is the actual unfinished work.

Constraints

  • Budget: Under $1,000 total. Soft ceiling — flag a decision that would exceed it rather than blocking on it.
  • Deadline / contest target: End of November 2026 (2–3 months). No contest; a write-up may come out of it later. Documentation quality is a goal in its own right.
  • Must use: Hardware already on hand — several DIYmalls ESP32-2432S028R displays, an AOC 16T20 monitor, a 24-inch vinyl cutter for panel templates and masks.
  • Must work from the right seat. An instructor sits there. Panel legibility and control reach from the right seat are requirements, not nice-to-haves. Affects panel geometry from Phase 3 onward: readable at an angle, not only dead-on from the left seat at my own eye height.
  • Must not: Rely on a display page knowing where its data came from. Transport stays separate from presentation so the simulator platform can change late.

Consequence of the right-seat requirement

Already relevant to hardware on hand:

  • The AOC 16T20 is IPS, which holds contrast off-axis. Good for the right seat.
  • The CYD's 2.8-inch panel is TN, which shifts noticeably off-axis. Anything an instructor must read is a poor fit for those boards.

Build

Phase 1 — Visual and embedded prototypes

Status: substantially complete

Four-page display prototype on the CYD

Firmware for the ESP32-2432S028R with four swipeable pages — engine monitor, DME and timer, electronic standby instrument, fuel and systems — driven by internally generated data. Flashed and running; touch, swiping and page wrap confirmed on the board.

Why this way: LovyanGFX rather than TFT_eSPI, because this board puts the touchscreen on a second SPI bus and TFT_eSPI's touch path assumes a shared one. Not LVGL, because these are custom aviation gauges rather than widgets — it would add a large dependency and a frame buffer the board has no room for, without helping draw a pitch ladder. Verified by: Flashed to hardware; display init, touch controller, and all page buffers reported healthy over serial. Swipe navigation and wrap exercised in both directions.

Off-device page renderer

A host-side tool that compiles the real page code and renders it into images on a desktop machine, without a board.

Why this way: Required changing every drawing function to take the base graphics type rather than the board's concrete device. Pages never called a board-specific method, so nothing was lost — and if one ever starts needing the board header, the preview stops building, which is a useful tripwire. Verified by: Renders 49 images covering every page. Found four defects on first use that no amount of reading the code had surfaced.

Six round instruments

A six-pack preview added to the firmware — airspeed, attitude, altimeter, turn coordinator, heading, vertical speed — bringing the board to ten pages.

Why this way: All six share a single 216×216 canvas. Six separate buffers would not fit; the board has no PSRAM and 116 KB of heap remains after allocation as it is. Verified by: 30-minute continuous soak on the attitude page — 40,135 renders, free heap identical at every five-minute sample.

Panel display measured and calibrated

A true-scale calibration target displayed on the AOC and measured with a ruler, establishing the real pixel density that every later panel dimension depends on.

Why this way: The density was previously assumed from the marketing screen size, which was 0.4% high. Measuring an 800-pixel bar removes the assumption; measuring the 3⅛-inch instrument dimension independently confirms the thing the project actually cares about. Verified by: Two independent ruler measurements agreeing — 140.7 PPI, and a 3⅛-inch instrument that measures 3⅛ inches on the glass.

Select X-Plane or MSFS, define the normalised aircraft-state schema, build the PC-side bridge, and drive the panel from live data. The firmware is already structured for this: pages consume an interface, so a real adapter replaces the generator without page changes.

Full-size vector drawing, tiled paper template, cardboard prototype, then a cut panel with the monitor behind it. Hole centres are already calculated for the measured display. Right-seat legibility gets checked at the cardboard stage, not after cutting.

Encoders for altimeter setting, heading bug and course. Switches, ignition, fuel selector. Annunciators and backlighting. Throttle, mixture and carburettor heat — no propeller control, the 152 has a fixed-pitch prop.

Final material and finish, permanent labels, serviceable harnesses, startup and shutdown behaviour, failure and disconnection handling, procedure-based test scenarios.

Results

  • Display firmware: ten pages on an ESP32-2432S028R, 26 KB static RAM, 116 KB free heap after all buffers allocate.
  • Endurance: 30 minutes continuous on the heaviest page, 40,135 renders, free heap identical at every five-minute sample. No leak, no drift, no crash.
  • Frame rate: 22–28 fps across the six round gauges, measured with on-device instrumentation. Below the 30 fps target; see Dead Ends for why it stays there.
  • Panel scale: 140.7 PPI measured, 13.65 × 7.68 in active area. A 3⅛-inch instrument renders at 3⅛ inches, confirmed by ruler twice.
  • Data model: 654,499 automated checks passing over three simulated hours — value ranges, derived-vs-independent quantities, and framerate independence.

What went wrong

80 MHz display SPI clock — 2026-08-22 to 08-24

  • Tried: Raising the display clock from 40 to 80 MHz to reach the 30 fps target. It worked on paper: every gauge hit a clean 30.2 fps, render times halved, heap stayed flat, and every serial diagnostic reported healthy.
  • Failed because: The panel visibly corrupted. 80 MHz is past the ILI9341's nominal write rate and these boards have long unshielded traces; the bus was mangling pixels in transit. Nothing on the device can see its own output, so no diagnostic could ever have caught it — it took a human looking at the screen.
  • Salvaged: The frame instrumentation built to measure it, which also caught an unverified 30 fps claim that was really 22. Written into the board config as a warning rather than a tunable, so the next person looking for speed doesn't repeat it.

Rendering the six gauges at a single instant — 2026-08-30

  • Tried: Previewing each gauge at one moment of the demo flight.
  • Failed because: That is the worst case for finding sign errors — every pointer sits mid-scale where being wrong doesn't show. Four real defects were hiding, including turn-coordinator index marks placed where the wingtip could never reach them.
  • Salvaged: The preview now samples seven points across the flight, putting each instrument somewhere revealing — airspeed below its scale, VSI at exactly zero and in both directions, turn coordinator left, right and centred.

What I'd do differently

  • Flash the board earlier. Three sessions of firmware were written and reviewed before anything ran on hardware. The board was plugged in the whole time.
  • Measure before assuming, every time. The display density, the frame rate, and the panel scale were all asserted from specifications before being measured. Two of the three were wrong — not badly, but wrong.
  • Update the front door when the baseline changes. The top-level README described the wrong aircraft for five days after the change, because the detailed docs were updated and the summary was not.
